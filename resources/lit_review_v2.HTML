1. Project Context and Purpose

This document is a literature review with a specific decision-making purpose: to inform the policies of the data preparation team — the project manager and linguistic advisor — on how to formulate questions, define answer quality, calibrate evaluation metrics, and guide annotators for the Hebrew Abstractive QA Dataset. It draws on eleven research sources and is filtered by the following use-case frame.

1.1 The use case

The dataset is not an educational tool. Its purpose is to benchmark LLM performance in an ad-hoc, document-grounded RAG scenario: a domain expert — a political analyst, a medical researcher, a legal advisor — receives documents they have not seen before, loads them into a model, and asks questions answerable only from those documents. They want to know whether the model gives the best possible answer: faithful to the source, without hallucination, and capable of synthesis when synthesis is required. The expert already has domain knowledge and will notice errors.

1.2 What the leaderboard diagnoses

The leaderboard answers one question: which models are trustworthy for expert RAG use? Leaderboard scores must be reported stratified by level (0–3). A model that retrieves facts reliably but fabricates at synthesis level is a different kind of risk from one that performs uniformly at integration level. That difference is what the leaderboard is designed to expose.

1.3 Why the educational framing in the sources does not transfer

The majority of the reviewed sources study Bloom's Taxonomy in formal education: automated question generation for classrooms, LLM tutoring systems, assessment of student learning outcomes. None of this is the project's use case. The sources are mined here exclusively for findings that transfer to the expert-RAG context:

  • How to prompt an LLM to generate questions at a specific cognitive level
  • How to distinguish a genuine synthesis question from an extractive one
  • How LLMs fail at higher-order tasks — and how that maps to answer quality risks
  • How evaluation metrics should be defined per cognitive level
  • How human annotators can reliably classify and judge cognitive-level items

Findings about classroom pedagogy, student cognitive development, and learning outcomes are not cited in this review.

2. The Four-Level Taxonomy: Bloom Adapted for RAG

The project uses a four-level question complexity taxonomy that draws on Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) but is defined entirely in terms of what a model must do with a document. The educational meaning of "cognitive level" does not apply; the operational meaning does.

LevelNameWhat the model must do with the document Closest Bloom analogCount in dataset
0 Unanswerable Recognise that the document does not contain the answer and state so accurately —50
1 Retrieval Locate and return a specific fact from a single sentence Remember50
2 Integration Connect 2–3 facts from different locations in the document Understand / Apply300
3 Synthesis & Abstraction Understand the document's overall argument; draw an inference or apply conclusions to a new scenario Analyze / Evaluate / Create600
Why Level 3 dominates the dataset (60%) Level 1 and 2 performance converges across models — the literature uniformly shows that differences are sharpest at synthesis level [LEE2025] [YADAV2025] [BARARI]. A 60% Level 3 weighting maximises the leaderboard's discriminating power for the expert-RAG scenario, where synthesis questions are exactly the ones the target user cannot easily answer themselves.

2.1 The human–LLM cognition gap and why it matters here

Bloom's Taxonomy was built on a theory of human metacognition. Applying it to LLMs introduces a structural mismatch: LLM training on next-token prediction maps naturally to lower-order levels, and the metacognitive level "has no real cognition to speak of" [HUBER2024]. "LLMs acquire knowledge through probabilistic associations rather than lived experience. Certain cognitive dimensions, particularly critical judgment or originality, may not directly translate from human cognition to machine cognition" [LEE2025].

This matters for two reasons. First, it explains why Level 3 performance is reliably the weakest across all studies — and therefore why the leaderboard will be most informative there. Second, it means that a model can produce text that looks like synthesis — coherent, relevant, well-organised — while merely recombining training patterns. The evaluation rubric must be designed to distinguish genuine inference from sophisticated collage. Put differently: the ambition of the leaderboard — to certify that a model is ready for every type of inquiry a domain expert might bring, not just fact retrieval — is precisely what the taxonomy's upper levels are designed to probe.

3. Policy 1 — Question Formulation

This section defines the policies for generating questions that span the full range of inquiry a domain expert might bring: from verifying a single fact (Level 1) through cross-document integration (Level 2) to questions that require the model to reason across an entire document and draw inferences the document implies but never states (Level 3). The 60% weighting at Level 3 is deliberate — it is precisely the inquiry type that resists retrieval and requires genuine cognitive engagement with the source material.

3.1 The information-seeking design principle is validated

The project's core generation methodology — showing the LLM only the first 100 characters of a document (TyDi QA excerpt-only approach) — is grounded in the right logic and independently supported by the literature. Yadav et al. found that designing each question as a single-turn prompt grounded only in the original context "isolates the effect of increasing cognitive demand alone, avoids error propagation, and ensures that each question cleanly tests a distinct cognitive skill" [YADAV2025]. This validates the project's single-question-per-document design.

3.2 Best prompting strategy for generation: CoT + level label + one worked example

Scaria et al. tested five prompting strategies across five LLMs [SCARIA2024]. The best combination — PS4: Chain-of-Thought + Bloom level description + one expert-crafted example question — achieved 81.37% question quality and 72.77% Bloom level adherence by human raters. This is the most rigorously documented prompt structure for cognitive-level question generation.

The over-specification failure mode PS5 — which added a full skill explanation on top of PS4 — degraded performance on both quality and level adherence across all models: "too much information in the prompt can be counterproductive" [SCARIA2024]. The rule: supply the level label and one example, not a lengthy description. This applies equally to prompts for Level 3 generation.
Policy Decision 3.2

Generation prompts for all levels should follow the PS4 structure: CoT instruction + explicit level label with brief definition + one worked example question from a curated set. Do not add extensive level explanations.

3.3 Explicitly state the target level — do not let the model infer it

Zoumpoulidi et al. compared two strategies [ZOUMPOULIDI]:

Baseline (no guidance)Question + answer, no Bloom promptN/A56.91

Explicitly providing the target level produces measurably better-aligned outputs. Every generation prompt should name the level and include its operational definition (not Bloom's original educational phrasing, but the project's RAG-task definition from §2).

3.4 Few-shot substantially outperforms zero-shot

Yadav et al. report that few-shot prompting "substantially outperforms zero-shot" for targeted level question generation [YADAV2025]. Senthilkumar & Nandhini observe that zero-shot generation "tends to copy source sentences" — producing items that are effectively extractive rather than information-seeking [SENTHILKUMAR]. The recommended configuration combines RAG-retrieved few-shot examples with the PS4 prompt structure, producing the best documented balance of quality, level adherence, and linguistic diversity.

3.5 Level 3 generation requires a mandatory human validation gate

Every source that measures across all Bloom levels finds Create/Synthesize questions the hardest to generate reliably:

  • [SCARIA2024]: "inability of most models to generate high-quality questions at the 'Create' level"
  • [ZOUMPOULIDI]: "performance declines as taxonomy level increases… LLMs generally struggle with abstract reasoning regardless of scale"
  • [LEE2025]: all models plateau at ~83–86% at Creating even with RAG
Policy Decision 3.5

Level 3 questions must not be passed from LLM generation to the dataset without a mandatory human validation step. Given that 600 of 1,000 questions are Level 3, this gate is the single highest-leverage quality control point in the pipeline. Consider requiring human drafting of the initial Level 3 question, using the LLM only for paraphrase variants.

3.6 Operational test: Level 2 vs Level 3 boundary

The Level 2/3 boundary is the most frequent annotator disagreement point [HUBER2024]. The following test should be the primary decision rule:

The single-paragraph test Ask: "Is there a paragraph in the document that, read in isolation, would answer this question?" If yes → Level 2 (or lower). If the document must be read as a whole to construct the answer → Level 3. A question that uses synthesis-sounding language ("how does X relate to Y?") but can be answered by copying two adjacent sentences is Level 2, not Level 3.
MarkerLevel 2 (integration)Level 3 (synthesis/abstraction)
Answer source2–3 explicit sentences, possibly in different paragraphsWhole-document argument; no single paragraph is sufficient
Answer typeCan be assembled from quoted spansCannot be fully quoted; requires reformulation
Inferential demand"Connect A to B as stated in the document""What does the combination of A, B, and C imply about X?"
New-scenario applicabilityNoAnswer applies to a scenario not mentioned in the document

3.7 Two-stage generation pipeline: draft with a weaker model, refine with a stronger one

Abdullah et al. [ABDULLAH2025] demonstrate a two-stage multi-LLM pipeline that addresses a consistent failure mode in single-pass generation: weaker models produce questions that are "simple, raw, and straightforward" even when prompted for higher cognitive levels. Their approach:

  1. Stage 1 — Draft. A smaller model (Llama-2-7b) generates a first-pass set of QA candidates from chunked source material. The draft questions are shallow but provide broad topical coverage of the source corpus.
  2. Stage 2 — Refine. A larger model (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct) with RAG retrieves relevant context from a curated knowledge base and restructures each draft question according to the target cognitive level. The refinement prompt explicitly supplies Bloom-level action verbs for each tier: define / identify / describe / summarise (Level 1); solve / implement / organise / analyse (Level 2); criticise / justify / design / compose (Level 3).

The concrete before/after contrast from their results illustrates what refinement achieves: a question like "What is the purpose of an adhesion promoter?" (Level 1, extractive) was restructured into "Explain the purpose of an adhesion promoter in context X and summarise the comparative benefits across scenarios A and B" — requiring multi-part reasoning and integration, closer to Level 2. The same mechanism, applied to Level 3 target verbs, produces questions requiring genuine synthesis.

Action verb scaffolding Adding a Bloom-level action verb list to the generation prompt — as a complement to the level label and worked example already prescribed in PS4 — gives the model a concrete linguistic scaffold for the question's cognitive demand. For Level 3, verbs like criticise, justify, synthesise, evaluate, compose signal the inferential depth without over-specifying the topic.
The mandatory human gate as the third pipeline stage The two-stage pipeline in Abdullah et al. remains fully automated, and the authors report no IAA data on Bloom-level adherence for their highest-difficulty outputs. The mandatory human validation gate prescribed in §3.5 functions as a third stage in this architecture — the stage that catches questions the refinement model has upgraded in surface form but not in genuine cognitive demand.
Policy Decision 3.7

Generation prompts should include cognitive-level action verbs alongside the level label and worked example (PS4 structure). For Level 3, include at least two verbs from the cluster synthesise / evaluate / justify / compose. The two-stage pipeline pattern (draft → refine with RAG) is a viable option for scaling generation, but the human validation gate remains mandatory for any Level 3 output regardless of pipeline configuration.

4. Policy 2 — Answer Formulation and Quality Standards

4.1 Hallucination is the baseline risk, not an edge case

Analysts estimate that LLMs hallucinate in as much as 27% of responses, with factual errors present in 46% of generated texts [TAYNTON]. The mechanism is structural: "LLMs are designed to generate text that is plausible-sounding and statistically aligned with learned patterns, rather than to verify the truthfulness of the information they produce" [TAYNTON]. This means linguistic coherence does not indicate factual accuracy. An answer that reads fluently and is well-organised may still contain fabricated facts.

Even RAG-augmented systems do not eliminate this: "hallucinations persist, particularly in applications where absolute accuracy and reliability are paramount" [TAYNTON]. For the expert-user scenario, this is precisely the failure mode that damages trust: a confident, well-written answer that the expert cannot immediately verify is more dangerous than an obviously incomplete one.

Annotation implication Annotators must not rely on fluency or coherence as a proxy for faithfulness. A high Coherence score is not evidence against hallucination. Attribution scoring is the primary safeguard: every claim must be verified against a specific span, not inferred from the answer's apparent plausibility.

4.2 RAG improves retrieval but does not reliably produce Level 3 synthesis

Lee et al. demonstrate that RAG significantly improves accuracy at Remembering, Understanding, and Analyzing, but "while retrieval mechanisms bolster reproduction and comprehension, they do not yet sufficiently support culturally nuanced generative synthesis" [LEE2025]. The model has the right raw material; it still may fail to integrate it into a novel conclusion. Level 3 answer quality cannot be guaranteed by RAG alone — the evaluation rubric must check for synthesis independently of factual grounding.

4.3 The extractive-collage failure mode

This is the critical failure mode for Level 3 that the standard five metrics do not catch. A model can achieve high scores on Coherence, Relevance, Completeness, Faithfulness, and Attribution by selecting and lightly reformulating the three or four most relevant paragraphs from the document. The result is factually correct, well-organised, and fully cited — but it has not synthesised. It has collaged.

"AI creativity may reflect pattern imitation rather than innovation" [BARARI]. LLMs exhibit "a tendency towards overfitting, leading to the regurgitation of standard solutions and a lack of innovation" [TAYNTON]. The Abstraction Quality metric (§5.5) is the mechanism for detecting this failure.

4.4 What a correct Level 3 answer looks like

A high-quality Level 3 answer:

  • Draws a conclusion that is not stated verbatim anywhere in the document
  • The conclusion is logically necessitated by the document's facts taken together
  • Names or describes the inferential step: "This implies…", "Given that X and Y, the main constraint is…", "These findings suggest that…"
  • Does not merely list document facts in order of document appearance

A Level 3 answer that should be rejected or scored low:

  • Every sentence has a direct 1:1 source span — indicates extraction, not synthesis
  • The answer is a well-organised summary of main points with no inferential conclusion
  • Uses the word "therefore" to connect sentences that were already adjacent in the source
  • Applies the word "synthesis" in its framing while producing extractive content

4.5 The Faithfulness redefinition at Level 3

The current Faithfulness score 2 definition — "no information beyond the document text" — is logically incompatible with Level 3. A correct Level 3 answer must contain inference that goes beyond any single sentence; that is the definition of synthesis. The following per-level redefinition is required:

ScoreLevels 1–2 meaningLevel 3 meaning (revised)
2 Every claim is supported by a direct span Every claim is either directly supported by a span, OR is a logical inference necessitated by the combination of document facts — no external knowledge imported
1 Minor unsupported additions present Reasonable inferences made, but one or two claims import plausible external knowledge not derivable from the document alone
0 Hallucinations or facts contradicting the document Claims that contradict the document, or introduce external facts that cannot be derived from it
Operational test for Level 3 Faithfulness score 2 "Could a careful reader independently reach this conclusion from the document, without any external knowledge?" If yes → score 2. If the answer requires external knowledge to follow or verify → score 1 or 0.

4.6 Level 0 (unanswerable) scoring decision table

The current guidelines note that Faithfulness and Relevance for Level 0 are assessed on "the correctness of the claim that there is no information," but do not specify how other metrics map. The following decision table resolves this:

4.7 Knowledge type as an orthogonal diagnostic dimension

The project's taxonomy stratifies questions by cognitive level (0–3). Abdullah et al. [ABDULLAH2025] demonstrate the value of an orthogonal dimension: knowledge type — factual, conceptual, or procedural — drawn from the revised Bloom's Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). A question at any given cognitive level can target any of these knowledge types, and performance failures at higher levels are often traceable to a specific knowledge-type gap rather than a uniform reasoning deficit.

Knowledge typeWhat the answer draws onExample at Level 3
Factual Discrete facts, named events, specific figures "What three specific decisions led to the outcome described?"
Conceptual Categories, principles, underlying tensions, generalisations "What structural tension does this testimony reveal about the policy goal?"
Procedural Sequences, methods, causal chains, decision steps "What sequence of steps does the document imply would be needed to implement this?"

Knowledge type is not a required tagging dimension in the current MAFAT specification. However, it is a useful diagnostic lens: a model that fails Level 3 questions targeting conceptual knowledge (abstract tensions, implications) but performs adequately on Level 3 questions targeting procedural knowledge reveals a specific reasoning gap, not a general Level 3 weakness. A future leaderboard extension could stratify scores by knowledge type within each level, producing a richer capability profile.

4.8 The highest cognitive tier formally resists automated evaluation: convergent evidence

Three independent sources now converge on the same conclusion from different directions (a fourth data point, Yudha et al., is in the §7 table):

  • [HUBER2024]: "None of the standard benchmarks cover the Create or Evaluate dimensions" — the field has not solved upper-level Bloom evaluation.
  • [LEE2025]: RAG-augmented models plateau at ~83–86% at Creating even with optimal retrieval — the performance ceiling is structural, not a tuning problem.
  • [ABDULLAH2025]: The Create/metacognitive dimension was explicitly excluded from their automated pipeline because "it would be challenging for the system to determine its correctness" — the authors could not design a computable correctness criterion for novel-synthesis questions.

This convergence matters because it reframes the Level 3 evaluation challenge. The difficulty is not transitional — it will not be resolved by the next generation of models. It is epistemological: the type of inquiry that requires genuine synthesis does not have a ground-truth answer that any automatic system can retrieve or verify. Human judgment is load-bearing at this level, not a temporary placeholder.

What this means for the annotator role The human annotator for Level 3 items is not a stopgap pending better automation. They are the irreplaceable arbiter for a class of questions that is formally non-automatable in the current state of the field. Communicating this to annotators — as the explicit rationale for the higher scrutiny and mandatory IAA checks at Level 3 — is both honest and motivating.

5. Policy 3 — Evaluation Metrics: Definitions, Scope, and a Sixth Metric

5.1 Where the five metrics come from

The five metrics — Coherence, Relevance, Completeness, Faithfulness, Attribution — are not from a single canonical standard. They represent a convergence of two literature streams that became the de-facto community practice for RAG answer evaluation by 2024:

  • Coherence, Relevance, Completeness trace to SummEval (Fabbri et al., 2021, TACL) and were operationalised for LLM-based evaluation by G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023, EMNLP).
  • Faithfulness is the central contribution of RAGAS (Es et al., 2023): "every factual claim must be supported by the retrieved context."
  • Attribution comes from ALCE (Gao et al., 2023, EMNLP), which introduced citation recall and precision as evaluatable dimensions for grounded generation.

These frameworks converged by 2024 into the roughly same five-metric bundle across evaluation toolkits (ARES, TruLens, DeepEval, Ragas v2). The MAFAT spec reflects this consensus faithfully. The metrics are well-chosen for Levels 0–2; the gap is structural at Level 3.

5.2 Why the five metrics are structurally limited at Level 3

All five metrics reward retrieval-grounded generation — staying close to retrieved passages, citing sources precisely, covering what the document says. This is exactly correct for Levels 0–2. At Level 3, the same reward function works against the goal: it gives maximum scores to a model that extracts and reformats document content without synthesising. The collage failure mode (§4.3) can score 2/2/2/2/2 on all five metrics.

Huber et al. document this at the benchmark level: the Create and Evaluate dimensions of Bloom's Taxonomy "lack usable metrics with upper bounds" — "ranking is relative to other models… this allows for a comparison between models but makes it difficult to measure absolute performance" [HUBER2024]. This is not a gap unique to this project; it is an open problem in the field.

Implication for leaderboard reporting Level 3 scores should be treated as comparative (which model scores higher than another on synthesis tasks) rather than absolute (a score of 1.8/2.0 means the answer is 90% synthetically correct). Reporting Level 3 scores as ranks or relative differences between models is more defensible than reporting them as absolute quality measures.

5.3 Revised metric definitions per level

Faithfulness

See §4.5. Definition splits at Level 3: score 2 permits grounded inference; score 0 targets external fabrication. The key operational test: can a careful reader reach this conclusion from the document alone?

Completeness

Levels 1–2: Current definition is correct — "covers all key information present in the document relevant to the question."
Level 3: Reframe as question-scoped, not document-exhaustive. "The answer represents the major lines of argument or evidence relevant to the question, and the inferential conclusion is supported by the main thrust of the document." Do not reward exhaustive enumeration — that would directly conflict with the Relevance penalty on verbosity.

Coherence and Relevance

Definitions hold across all levels. Annotators must be explicitly warned: a high score on both Coherence and Relevance is not evidence of synthesis. The collage failure mode scores 2/2 on both. These metrics cannot substitute for Abstraction Quality at Level 3.

Attribution

Definition holds across all levels with one Level 3 addition: it is acceptable to attribute an inferential claim to a combination of spans (e.g., "Based on paragraphs A and B, the implication is…"). The inference itself, not the literal claim, is what is being attributed to the span combination. Score 2 if the inference is clearly grounded in the cited spans; score 1 if the connection is vague or requires external knowledge to follow.

5.4 LLM-as-judge: scope and limits

LLM-as-judge achieves approximately 90% agreement with human judgements "in structured contexts" [TAYNTON]. However, performance is "highly dependent on the clarity and consistency of evaluation criteria" and LLMs "struggle with subjective or ambiguous distinctions" [TAYNTON]. Separately, Scaria et al. found that LLM judges are unreliable for Bloom-level assessment, systematically misclassifying toward Apply/Analyze [SCARIA2024].

These two findings are compatible. The recommended scope:

5.5 A sixth metric: Abstraction Quality (Level 3 only)

The gap in the current five-metric scheme is the absence of a metric that directly assesses whether the model synthesised rather than compiled. Huber et al. note the field has not solved this [HUBER2024]; Barari et al. and Taynton describe the failure mode precisely [BARARI] [TAYNTON]. The following metric is proposed for Level 3 items only:

ScoreDescriptionDiagnostic marker
0 Extractive. Sentences are lifted or lightly paraphrased; no integration. Every sentence in the answer has a direct 1:1 source span
1 Reformulative. Answer organises the document's content around the question, but draws no new inference. Synthesis of structure, not of meaning; no sentence expresses something the document implies but never states
2 Inferential. Integrates evidence and draws a conclusion not stated explicitly in any single sentence, but logically necessitated by the document as a whole. At least one sentence expresses something the document implies but never states; the conclusion could not be found by searching any single paragraph
Scope: Level 3 only Abstraction Quality should be scored N/A for Levels 0–2. Level 1 and 2 answers are explicitly expected to stay close to source text; scoring them on abstraction would penalise correct behaviour.

6. Policy 4 — Annotator Guidance

6.1 Question validation: the cascading 9-item rubric

Scaria et al. developed and validated a hierarchical rubric for question quality that is directly applicable to human validation of LLM-generated questions [SCARIA2024]. Its key feature is a cascade: a question failing an early criterion is not evaluated on later ones, reducing annotation effort without sacrificing coverage:

StepCriterionOutcome if failed
1Understandable? Can you understand what the question is asking?Discard
2TopicRelated? Is it related to the document topic?Discard
3Grammatical?Discard
4Clear? Is it clear what is being asked?"More or less" → require rephrase; "No" → discard
5Answerable? Can it be answered from the document?Discard or re-level
6Central? Is answering this question important to the topic?Discard
7WouldYouUseIt? Would you use this question if you were evaluating an LLM on this document?"No" → discard; "Maybe" → review
8Level? Assign Level 0–3Assign; flag borderline L2/3 for peer review

IAA results with this rubric: κ = 0.83–0.93 for Level assignment with the PS4 prompt, supporting the project's 0.9 IAA target [SCARIA2024]. Agreement is highest for objective criteria (Grammatical: κ = 1.00; TopicRelated: κ = 0.93–0.99) and lowest for subjective ones (Rephrase: κ = 0.53–0.76). Annotator calibration training should focus specifically on Level boundary judgment (L2/L3) and Rephrase.

6.2 Do not use a general-purpose LLM as the sole Level classifier

Scaria et al. found that Gemini Pro used as an automated judge "revealed a tendency to classify most machine-generated questions as belonging to the 'Apply' or 'Analyze' levels regardless of actual level… extreme caution must be exercised when using LLMs for automated evaluation" [SCARIA2024].

This means LLM-based Level pre-screening can flag obvious Level 1 (extractive) questions as a first-pass filter, but cannot certify Level 3 classification. Human adjudication is required for all final Level assignments. If automated assistance is needed, a task-specific fine-tuned classifier substantially outperforms a prompted general LLM: Bloomify (Mistral 7B + QLoRA) achieved MOS 9/10 for Bloom-level classification from domain experts [ATRE].

6.3 Bloom taxonomy boundaries are genuinely fuzzy

"The boundaries between the levels of Bloom's Taxonomy are fuzzy, and in practice individual tasks fall somewhere in between two levels" [HUBER2024]. This is not an annotator training failure; it is intrinsic to the framework. The literature reports it uniformly. The mitigation is the single-paragraph test (§3.6) as a decision rule, plus explicit calibration sessions on borderline L2/L3 cases before main annotation begins.

6.4 LLM-as-judge scope for answer scoring

See §5.4 for the full scope table. The minimum human coverage specified in the MAFAT doc (IAA check on 35% of answers) is appropriate, but Level 3 items should be over-represented in that 35% sample given that Completeness, Abstraction Quality, and the Level 3 Faithfulness redefinition all require human judgment.

7. The Higher-Order Performance Ceiling: Convergent Evidence

The most consistent finding across all reviewed sources — across domains, models, and methodologies — is a systematic performance decline at higher Bloom levels. This is not a dataset artefact or a tuning problem; it is a structural characteristic of how LLMs process information. It is the core reason the leaderboard is most informative at Level 3, and it directly answers the question of whether current LLMs are ready for the full range of inquiry a domain expert might bring: they are not, and the evidence is robust and domain-independent.

SourceDomainFinding at synthesis level
[YADAV2025] In-car AI assistant 52% correct at Level 1 (Remember); only 6% at Level 6 (Create)
[SCARIA2024] Question generation "Inability of most models to generate high-quality Create-level questions"
[MA2025] Code repair Remember: 53–82% plausible patches; Analyze (real-world): 13–41%
[LEE2025] Cultural knowledge QA with RAG All models plateau ~83–86% at Creating even with RAG augmentation
[ZOUMPOULIDI] STEM QA generation "Performance declines as taxonomy level increases"
[BARARI] Engineering domain QA All three LLMs decrease at higher levels; "AI creativity may reflect pattern imitation"
[HUBER2024] NLP benchmark meta-analysis No standard benchmark covers Create or Evaluate; these "lack usable metrics with upper bounds"
[YUDHA2026] Adaptive essay assessment system System explicitly limited to Levels 1–2 (Remembering, Understanding); analysis and synthesis named as beyond scope and future work

7.1 Model choice matters more than cognitive level

Lee et al. find via ANOVA that model choice is a more significant predictor of performance than cognitive level: F(5,30) = 15.81 (significant) for model vs. F(6,30) = 2.26, p = 0.065 (not significant) for Bloom level [LEE2025]. This is the leaderboard's key practical implication: when the dataset is used to rank models, cross-model differences will dominate over within-model level variation. The leaderboard will primarily tell you which model is better for expert RAG, with Level 3 performance as the most discriminating sub-score.

7.2 Implication for the dataset distribution

The 60% Level 3 weighting is defensible on exactly these grounds: it is the level where model differences are largest, where the expert-user scenario is most relevant, and where current LLM capabilities are most uncertain. The 5% Level 0 and 5% Level 1 serve as calibration anchors — models that fail at Level 1 retrieval are unreliable at all levels; models that fail at Level 0 refusal introduce a trust-critical hallucination risk.

8. Benchmark Contamination and the Hebrew Safeguard

Ma et al. demonstrate that LLMs perform significantly better on static benchmark datasets than on genuinely novel tasks at the same stated cognitive level: "Bloom-layered dynamic evaluation reveals true capability" that is hidden by static benchmarks [MA2025]. Accuracy swings of up to 25 percentage points from answer-ordering effects and 20–30 pp from irrelevant distractors show how sensitive benchmark scores are to surface features [TAYNTON].

The Hebrew safeguard This project's use of Hebrew-language sources — Knesset protocols, Israel HaYom, and Hebrew Wikipedia — provides a natural and strong safeguard against training-data contamination. These materials are substantially underrepresented in the training corpora of major LLMs compared to English sources, making genuine-capability testing much more likely. The Knesset protocols in particular — spoken formal Hebrew with political and connotative register — are unlikely to appear in any model's training data in a form that would allow memorisation of specific passages. The novelty of the material is itself a methodological strength: because the model cannot rely on training-set exposure, every question — at every level — genuinely tests the model's capacity to process an unfamiliar document and handle the type of inquiry the question presents. This is the closest available approximation to the domain-expert use case this dataset is designed to evaluate.

8.1 The flip side: cross-lingual embedding degradation is a real evaluation risk

Hebrew is an asset for contamination resistance, but it introduces a technical risk on the evaluation side. Yudha et al. [YUDHA2026] provide empirical measurement of exactly this effect in a closely analogous situation: they compared embedding-based semantic similarity scores for the same questions and answers in English (the original source language) vs. Indonesian (a translated version).

ConditionCosine similarity rangeStability
Original language (English) 0.85–0.92 Higher and more stable; consistent relevance pattern
Translated version (Indonesian) 0.76–0.89 Lower floor; drops when vocabulary doesn't fully translate (e.g. technical terms)

The root cause: "text embedding could not fully capture cross-lingual semantic relevance" — the embedding space is trained predominantly on English, so comparisons that cross language boundaries are less reliable. The Pearson correlation between question similarity and answer similarity (0.76) was the same in both languages, meaning the relative pattern is preserved but the absolute scores are systematically lower and more variable in the non-English condition.

Project implication: calibrate embedding-based metrics for Hebrew The evaluation pipeline for this project uses RAGAS Faithfulness, which is embedding-based (it checks whether claims in the answer are supported by the retrieved context using semantic similarity). If the embedding model used is English-dominant (e.g. ada-002, text-embedding-3, or similar), Hebrew-language comparisons will produce systematically lower similarity scores — not because the answers are less faithful, but because the embedding space is less reliable for Hebrew. Two practical implications:
  • Calibrate annotation thresholds using Hebrew pilot data, not English-derived defaults. A "good" Faithfulness score in Hebrew may sit lower on the raw cosine scale than the same quality answer in English.
  • Consider using a multilingual or Hebrew-specialised embedding model (e.g. LaBSE, multilingual-e5, or a Hebrew-tuned model from Dicta) for Faithfulness and Attribution scoring. This is particularly important at Level 3, where the semantic gap between the answer and its source spans is inherently wider.

This finding also strengthens the Open Question about Hebrew-language generalisability (§9): it is not only that prompting strategies and IAA results may differ in Hebrew — the evaluation metrics themselves may behave differently, and that needs to be empirically checked before relying on absolute score thresholds.

The leaderboard should report performance broken down by Level (0–3), not just as an aggregate. A Bloom-stratified score exposes the differential pattern documented across all reviewed sources — strong at Level 1, weaker at Level 3 — which is precisely where genuine capability differences between models become visible.

9. Open Questions and Limitations

  • Hebrew-language generalisability. All reviewed studies were conducted in English (or Korean cultural content in English). It is unknown whether the same prompting strategies, IAA levels, and performance patterns hold for Hebrew — a morphologically rich language with different tokenisation characteristics and substantially less LLM training data. Crucially, §8.1 documents a concrete risk: cross-lingual embedding degradation means that embedding-based evaluation metrics (RAGAS Faithfulness, semantic similarity scoring) may produce systematically lower and less stable scores in Hebrew than the field's English-derived calibration data would suggest [YUDHA2026]. Hebrew-specific calibration of score thresholds is required before the metric system can be deployed reliably.
  • No resolved metric for synthesis quality. Huber et al. note that the field has not yet proposed "a principled adaptation from human to LLM cognition" [HUBER2024]. The proposed Abstraction Quality metric (§5.5) is grounded in the literature's diagnostic findings but has not been independently validated. It should be piloted on the first 50-question batch and IAA-checked before deployment. Abdullah et al. [ABDULLAH2025] reach the same conclusion from a different direction: when building a full automated QA pipeline they found the Create/metacognitive tier formally unassessable, excluding it entirely — "the most prominent [limitation] is the removal of the metacognitive dimension… it is not feasible to assess these abilities automatically." This is independent confirmation that Abstraction Quality addresses a genuinely unsolved problem, not a solved-but-unadapted one.
  • Level 3 scores are relative, not absolute. Without an upper bound on synthesis metrics, the leaderboard's Level 3 scores are comparative rankings, not absolute capability measures. Future work may develop better upper-bound criteria. One direction worth tracking: Verga et al. (2024, cited in [ABDULLAH2025]) propose "jury-based" evaluation in which a panel of diverse LLM judges vote on a response rather than relying on a single model, reducing individual model bias and producing more reliable consensus on borderline cases. Applied to Abstraction Quality scoring, a jury of diverse models might yield more stable relative rankings at Level 3 than any single automated judge — though it does not resolve the absolute upper-bound problem.
  • Model temporal drift. LLM performance patterns can be partly explained by memorisation of training data. As models are retrained, static benchmark comparisons become unreliable over time [MA2025]. The leaderboard will need periodic re-evaluation with held-out question sets.
  • RAG configuration effects. Lee et al.'s ANOVA finding that model choice dominates over cognitive level may vary with RAG configuration. The leaderboard should document the retrieval setup for each model evaluation.
